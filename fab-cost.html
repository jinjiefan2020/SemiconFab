Building a Fab: Where the Money Goes Beyond the Equipment

Process tools account for 70–80% of the total investment in an advanced fab. The remaining 20–30% — building, cleanroom, ultrapure water, power, gases, wastewater, AMHS, land, and engineering design — is the so-called "construction cost". This page brings together the shares, unit prices, and reference projects that can be found in public sources into a parametric model that estimates cost by process node × capacity (WSPM), and states plainly which figures come from official sources, which are triangulated across multiple sources, and which cannot be found at all.

Sources: public web research conducted on 2026-10-18 (trade media, practitioner interviews, analyst estimates, and company announcements). All amounts are in US dollars. No institution (SEMI / McKinsey / BCG / SIA) has ever published a complete cost breakdown in public channels — the breakdown below is triangulated across multiple sources and is the weakest link in this data; read it with that caveat.

Parametric Estimator

Method: total investment = cost per 1k WSPM (process anchor) × capacity; non-equipment construction cost = total investment × facility share. All anchors are published figures from the "Node scaling" table below; the facility share uses the report's 20–30% range (the upper bound can be relaxed to Samsung Taylor's official 35% building share). This is a rough model, not a quotation.

The report gives no per-1k-WSPM cost anchor for 7nm, so no 7nm option is offered — do not fabricate data by interpolation. IBS figures are considered high by SemiWiki practitioners; for the same 2nm node there is also Samsung's published $340M figure, listed side by side for comparison.

Model limitations (from the report): ① No parametric capacity curve (cost ∝ WSPMx) exists in public sources; this model extrapolates total cost linearly with capacity — but the facility portion actually scales sub-linearly (a UPW plant, substation, or CUB does not double with capacity), so the facility share will be underestimated for small fabs and overestimated for large ones; a phase-2 fab on the same campus shares infrastructure and costs significantly less. ② Land (~3–5%, $20M–200M, 100–500 acres; weak source quality) and engineering / permitting / commissioning (~3–8%) are not itemized separately and are contained within the uncertainty range of the facility share. ③ The ~+30% per-node rule of thumb (construction-physics) is driven mainly by more expensive tools and more mask / process steps, and lands on the equipment side.

Node Scaling: Total Cost per 1k WSPM

On a consistent basis (total investment incl. equipment), node is the second-largest variable — the largest is actually logic vs. memory: advanced DRAM costs less than half per 1k WSPM ($69M) of mature 28nm logic ($156M). Rule of thumb: each node advance adds ~30% to total cost.

Capacity Scaling and Back-End (OSAT)

Capacity: equipment linear, facilities sub-linear

  • Equipment scales roughly in proportion to wafer starts at a given node (double the capacity ≈ double the equipment), so the 70–80% equipment share broadly holds across fab sizes.
  • Facilities show real but limited economies of scale: a UPW plant, ASU, substation, and CUB do not double with WSPM. Indirect evidence: Kumamoto Fab 1 at $156M per 1k WSPM, versus the unconfirmed Fab 2 at ~$1.1B/1k — small fabs carry far higher unit facility costs.
  • A phase-2 fab on the same campus is significantly cheaper: shared power, water, logistics, permits, and talent pool (GF Malta case; both Kumamoto Fab 2 and Samsung Taylor Fab 2 sit on existing campuses).
  • Part of the driver of giant fabs has nothing to do with cost: utilization headroom, fewer managers, faster yield learning, fewer financing rounds (chiphistory.org).
  • Modeling suggestion: total cost = campus fixed items (land, substation, UPW, CUB — sub-linear) + cleanroom / facility items linear in WSPM + equipment (linear). No parametric curve (e.g., cost ∝ WSPM0.x) has ever been published in public sources — this is an explicit data gap.

OSAT / back-end packaging & test plants

  • Reference anchor: Amkor's Arizona advanced packaging campus grew from a $2B plan to $7B (before the first building produced; broke ground 2025, production early 2028), covering advanced packaging + test, not traditional packaging alone (3dincites.com, Oct 2026).
  • Cost drivers: high-end testers cost several million dollars each (wevolver.com); fine-pitch RDL lithography and precision bonding are the other two major capital items. 2025 bonding equipment market structure: wire bonding 38.5% ($3.54B), die bonding 31.2% (dataintelo).
  • Equipment share is higher than in front-end fabs: ASE's 2025 ATM revenue mix was packaging 81%, test 18.5% (dataintelo), and test is precisely the capital-intensive bottleneck.
  • Scale reference: Taiwan test house Sigurd raised 2025 capex to NT$8.8B (~$270M) for test capacity expansion, including a new plant (asiadaily.org).
  • Model magnitudes: traditional packaging/test plant $0.5–2B; advanced packaging (CoWoS / fan-out) campus $2–7B. "Equipment ≈ 60–75% of OSAT capex" is an estimate backed out from cost drivers, not a directly sourced figure — cite it as such. No unit-capacity cost found (e.g., dollars per million units / month).

Timeline: Time Itself Is Cost

Typical durations for permitting + design → first wafer out (Blaschitz / Exyte·M+W, via TechSpot, 2025). Building a fab in the US costs 30% (Intel basis) to 4× (TSMC basis) more than in Taiwan, with a schedule roughly twice as long; a substantial part of the premium is "time-driven" — labor, extended site management costs, a ~12,000-person construction workforce (TSMC Arizona). Kumamoto Fab 1 compressed its schedule with 24/7 construction, but no public source quantifies the expediting premium (dollars per month saved) — not found.

Later stages

  • Tool install → acceptable yield: a further 6–12 months of ramp (construction-physics, 2024).
  • First wafer out → volume production: a further 6–18 months, depending on node maturity (e.g., VSMC risk production Sep 2026 → volume production Q1 2027).
  • A large fab typically needs 2–5 years from groundbreaking to full capacity (e.g., SK hynix Y2 planned groundbreaking Jul 2027 → first cleanroom ready Jun 2029).

Where the regional gap actually is

SK hynix's schedule (groundbreaking → first cleanroom in just 23 months) shows cleanroom shell construction speeds are similar across regions; the US gap lies mainly in permitting, labor supply, and the tool-install / qualification ecosystem, not in pouring concrete. In other words, the first lever for lowering US fab costs is process and ecosystem, not building unit prices.

Unverified / Gaps in the Public Data

The following questions have no reliable public answer from this round of research. Any model claiming to answer them precisely should be asked for its sources.

  1. No authoritative breakdown table (in public channels): no SEMI / McKinsey / BCG / SIA table of "equipment / building / facilities / land / other" percentages was found; this page's breakdown is triangulated from second-hand sources of uneven quality.
  2. Capacity undisclosed by several makers: WSPM for Intel Ohio and Micron Boise / Hiroshima / New York is not published, so unit-capacity costs cannot be computed.
  3. No parametric capacity curve: no published cost ∝ WSPMx curve exists — only qualitative evidence.
  4. Standalone substation cost; standalone chemicals / wastewater cost: no standalone figures found for either — only combined bases.
  5. OSAT unit-capacity cost (e.g., dollars per million units / month): not found.
  6. Expediting premium (dollars per month saved): not found.
  7. Official equipment-vs-building splits have nearly vanished: treated as company secrets; Samsung Taylor's $6B / $11B is the only official announced split.
  8. Some 2026 news figures come from low-quality aggregators (e.g., TSMC's $265B total, Samsung's $37B regional spend): flagged in the tables above — treat with caution; the core anchors (Wikipedia, company announcements, practitioner interviews, IBS) are relatively stronger.
